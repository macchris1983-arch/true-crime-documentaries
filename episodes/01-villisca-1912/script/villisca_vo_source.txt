=== CHUNK 00a ===
Eight people went to bed in one house in Villisca, Iowa.
By morning, all eight were dead.
No one was ever convicted.
But the strangest thing about this case
is not what went missing.
It's what kept showing up.

=== CHUNK 00c ===
This is the Villisca axe murder case.
June, nineteen twelve.
Joe and Sarah Moore, their four children,
and two young girls who stayed the night.
I'm not going to tell you who did it.
I'm going to show you the record.
Court testimony, and the newspapers of the time.
What was found in that house.
And everything that was added over the next nineteen years.
Welcome to story timez.
If you like your true crime told from the record,
subscribe,
and tell me in the comments which case I should open next.

=== CHUNK 00d ===
Everyone who tells this story
will tell you why it was never solved.
A crowd walked through the rooms.
The evidence was lost.
And that is partly true.
But here is what the paperwork shows.
This case did not run out of evidence.
For nineteen years...
it kept getting more.
A closet.
A photograph, said to come from inside a child's eye.
A sound that turned into a man.
A signed confession.
A fingerprint.

=== CHUNK 01 ===
On the morning of June tenth, nineteen twelve,
a mother in southwest Iowa
telephoned a neighbor's house.
Her husband told the coroner about it.
My wife did, yes.
He could not say what time.
She tried, he said,
about three different times.
Her two daughters had stayed the night there.
She expected them back.
[pause]
That house was locked.
The blinds were down.
I can't tell you who killed eight people in Villisca.
No one can.
What I can give you is an inventory.
What was in that house.
And what was carried in afterward.

=== CHUNK 02 ===
The first entry is the people.
Joe Moore was forty-three.
He sold farm implements.
Sarah Moore was thirty-nine,
and she helped run the Children's Day program
at the Presbyterian church.
Herman was eleven.
That Sunday night,
he spoke a piece
about God watching over the sparrows.
Katherine was ten.
Boyd was seven.
Paul was five.
Lena Stillinger took part in the program too.
Her sister Ina was with her.
The records disagree about their ages.
Lena was eleven or twelve.
Ina was eight or nine,
and one trial report says six.
At six o'clock that evening,
according to their older sister Blanche,
Joe Moore telephoned the Stillinger home.
He did not want the girls walking back in the dark.
Could they stay with his family?
Blanche said she thought that would be all right.
The pastor testified the program ended about nine fifteen.
Nobody who saw what came next
ever gave an account that held.
So I won't give you one.

=== CHUNK 03 ===
Monday morning.
A neighbor, Mary Peckham,
noticed how still the house was.
She tried the doors.
Joe's brother Ross came with a key.
He opened the door to the room off the parlor.
He told the coroner
he did not wait long enough to see anything else.
The town marshal, Hank Horton, went through every room.
And then he did something
almost nobody retells.
According to the Moore store clerk,
he locked the house
and left the keys with the neighbor.
Remember that.
A locked house.
A woman next door, holding the key.
It is the last moment anyone could say
who had been inside.
When the doctors went in,
every face was covered.
Dr. Williams climbed the stairs
to the room on the south side.
A small boy, on his stomach.
A gauze undershirt
laid over the top of his head.
[low voice] Dr. Williams told the inquest what he did next.
[pause]
I lifted that off...
lifted it off...
to see which one it was.

=== CHUNK 04 ===
So here is the first inventory.
An ax, near the bed where Lena and Ina were found.
A piece of bacon, wrapped in a flour sack,
at the head of that bed.
A lamp on the floor, at the foot of the parents' bed.
The marshal moved it out of the way.
Skirts and aprons hung over the windows.
Marks in the ceiling.
Dr. Williams found no footprints.
Five years later,
the coroner testified
there were no finger marks on the ax handle.
Then the house was opened again.
The National Guard came to hold the line.
One account says ten thirty.
Another says noon.
How many people went in before that, I can't tell you.
Later accounts say one hundred.
Others say hundreds.
I have not found a count from that day.
What the record does show
is a house entered again and again.
Jessie Moore testified she went in that day,
to get family pictures for the press.
Bedding was burned.
The house was cleaned, then repapered.
So yes.
Things were lost.
But loss only explains what disappeared.
It cannot explain what showed up.

=== CHUNK 05 ===
Item one arrives five days after the murders.
A newspaper report, datelined Omaha.
A woman named Retta Johnson
has come back from Villisca.
There is a closet, she says,
off the room where the Stillinger girls slept.
Bags of cotton batting inside.
On them, the marks of a man
who had sat and stood there.
And in the girls' bed,
a piece of watch chain.
Now follow the closet.
By the trial, five years on,
the list of exhibits names a closet upstairs.
A public comment posted under historian Edgar Epperly's name
traces a version of the story to a Kansas City reporter,
with cigarettes, and a heel print in the dust.
It says an undertaker checked,
and the box of cotton sat on two suit boxes
that had not been crushed.
Today, you will hear it was the attic.
Now follow the chain.
A watch chain, in a bed, in June.
By the trial,
a piece of key chain,
and Joe Moore's watch, on the floor.
The containers stayed the same.
What was inside them kept changing.

=== CHUNK 06 ===
August, nineteen twelve.
A man from Villisca tells people in Council Bluffs
that detectives have a photograph of the killer.
They got it, he says,
from the retina of one of the Stillinger sisters.
I have found no such photograph in any record.
Her parents were alive to read that.
By the end of that year,
newspapers had reported at least five different men held or arrested.
A brother-in-law.
A man with stained shoes.
A farmhand who wrote about a vision.
A Sioux City porter,
whom the headline identified by his race.
A farmer.
None of them was convicted of anything at Villisca.
The question had stopped being what happened in the house.
It had become
who was going to pay for it.

=== CHUNK 07 ===
In nineteen sixteen,
a private detective named James Wilkerson
brought the case a theory.
State senator Frank Jones,
Joe Moore's old employer and business rival,
had hired a killer.
Wilkerson's man was William Mansfield.
A grand jury refused to indict him.
Frank Jones was never charged with anything.
He sued Wilkerson for slander,
asked for sixty thousand dollars,
and lost.
And in that lawsuit,
a witness named Ed Landers took the oath.
You have met Landers already,
in a way.
He was staying a few houses from the Moores.
At the inquest in nineteen twelve,
he described something he heard that Sunday night.
A sound.
Like one boy hooting for another.
The next day, he said, it occurred to him
it might have been a woman moaning.
In the published summary of that testimony,
that is all there is.
Four years later,
under oath,
he said that on that same night
he saw Frank Jones's son, Albert,
go into the Moore home.
[pause]
Albert Jones was never charged either.
Both men denied any part in it.
The prosecution attacked Landers's account in open court.
[low voice] A sound...
became a man.

=== CHUNK 08 ===
In nineteen seventeen, the state found its own document.
The Reverend Lyn George Kelly
was a traveling preacher,
in town for one Sunday.
He was charged with the murder of Lena Stillinger.
He had been charged with mailing obscene letters,
and he had been held in a federal mental hospital.
He caught the five nineteen train that morning.
The train left hours before anyone opened the door.
Witnesses swore he spoke of the murders
before the bodies were found.
They swore it five years later.
A station ticket record backed up the date of one encounter.
It could not record what was said.
Then, Logan jail.
August thirtieth.
A newspaper editor testified
that he was handcuffed and placed in Kelly's cell,
playing an automobile thief.
A sheriff read out accusations.
The editor recalled eighty-seven paragraphs.
Kelly said,
that's a lie.
Early the next morning, a county attorney took down a statement.
Its first lines say it was made
without any promise or threats.
In it, a voice tells Kelly to slay utterly.
He climbs the stairs, thinking they are Jacob's ladder.
He kills the children first, it says.
Then the parents.
No record I have read can tell you
whether that is the order it happened.
The attorney general told the jury
Kelly had been asked fifteen times
whether he killed the parents first.
And fifteen times, he said no.
Twenty-five times, he was told no promise could be made.
Fifty-one times, he was told to tell the truth.
The state counted those.
It offered them as proof that he was free.
At trial, Kelly said
I have no recollection of signing that paper.
The record shows a signature.
It cannot show you what the signature is worth.

=== CHUNK 09 ===
There is one more name in the Omaha paper.
A committee was raising money for Kelly's defense.
It held meetings.
It passed the hat.
Its officers are listed.
Remember the man who told the coroner
my wife did, yes.
She phoned about three different times.
The state of Iowa had charged Kelly
with killing his daughter.
[pause]
And Joseph Stillinger
was the president of the committee
trying to set him free.

=== CHUNK 10 ===
The paper does not say why.
The committee said it wanted the guilty man brought to justice.
It did not believe it had him.
The first jury took twenty ballots.
Eleven for acquittal, every time.
One man, W. C. Brown, held for not guilty by reason of insanity.
In November, a second jury acquitted Kelly.
He was found not guilty.
That is the verdict.
There is one last item.
March, nineteen thirty-one.
A man in a Detroit jail,
after five hours with detectives,
confesses to the Villisca murders.
The papers report
that his fingerprints matched prints found at the scene.
Remember the coroner.
No finger marks on the handle.
And this man's confession has a problem.
He says he killed a man,
his wife,
and their four children.
Six people.
Officers said it did not tally.
[low voice] The last confession in the file...
forgot Lena and Ina.

=== CHUNK 11 ===
So here is the inventory now.
A house.
An ax.
A few photographs.
Everything else was carried in.
Some of it, by people with something to gain.
Much of it, I think,
by people who could not stand an empty line on the form.
That is what this kind of crime does.
It leaves a space the exact size of a name.
And a town will put anything in it.
A stranger.
A senator.
A preacher who heard voices.
No one has ever been convicted of these murders.
Reverend Kelly was acquitted.
Frank and Albert Jones were never charged.
The one thing in this file
that nobody needed to invent
is a mother,
on a Monday morning,
calling a house.
About three different times.
[pause]
This film is drawn from court testimony and newspapers of the time.
A report is not proof.
Everyone named here is presumed innocent of these murders.
